# ABOUT THIS GUIDE

Getting better at basketball is not about collecting moves.

It is about learning how to train, understand the game, take care of your body, compete and turn more and more actions into reliable habits.

This guide brings together thirty ideas to help you do that.

Some responsibilities change depending on the position you play. A point guard, shooting guard, small forward, power forward and center do not need to build exactly the same game or develop it in exactly the same order.

But some principles belong to everyone.

Your position guides your priorities.

It does not determine your ceiling.

This guide is not a substitute for your coaching staff or for qualified professionals in medicine, physiotherapy, strength and conditioning, psychology or nutrition. Whenever a decision requires individual knowledge of your health or physical situation, it belongs to qualified professionals who know your case.

You do not need to turn this guide into another list of obligations.

Use it to understand your development better.

# CHAPTER 1 · BUILD RELIABILITY

## 1. YOUR STARTING POINT

You come to basketball with a particular combination of experience, physical qualities, skills, habits and room to improve.

Some of those qualities may create opportunities for you.

None of them plays the game for you.

Height does not defend.

Speed does not make decisions.

Strength does not know the systems.

Experience does not guarantee concentration.

Talent does not arrive on time.

Your position does not decide how much you will improve either.

**What you do with your qualities matters more than simply having them.**

You do not need to show everything you can do straight away.

Your first objective can be much more specific:

> **Become someone your coaching staff can trust.**

Someone who understands their role.

Who competes.

Who listens.

Who reduces avoidable mistakes.

Who is available.

Who learns.

Who improves week after week.

Your role on the court will influence the first responsibilities you need to master.

The principle is the same for everyone:

**build trust first.**

---

## 2. THE MAIN RULE: BE AVAILABLE

Training a lot is not much use if you cannot train afterwards.

Your goal is not to prove every day how much you can tolerate.

Your goal is to be able to train **tomorrow, the day after tomorrow and six months from now**.

Learn to notice how you arrive at each session.

You can ask yourself:

- Did I sleep well?
- Am I unusually tired?
- Do I feel pain or discomfort that is different from normal?
- Does any area feel especially overloaded?
- Do I feel in my normal condition to train?

This is not about obsessively analysing every sensation.

It is about knowing your body and communicating what matters.

### Fundamental rule

**Pain, swelling, significant discomfort or a feeling of instability are not tests of character. They are information.**

Communicate them.

If you are recovering from an injury or following a medical, physiotherapy or return-to-sport process, decisions about workload and progression belong to the professionals responsible for your case.

Do not increase or modify a recovery process on your own.

Being available is something you train too.

---

## 3. YOUR FIRST MAJOR GOAL: BE RELIABLE

When you join a team or move to a new level, it is easy to feel that you need to show everything you can do immediately.

You do not.

First show that you can consistently do what the team needs from you.

Being reliable means the coaching staff can expect certain things when you are on the court:

- you know your responsibilities;
- you compete;
- you occupy space well;
- you defend according to the plan;
- you communicate;
- you make simple, consistent decisions;
- you reduce avoidable mistakes;
- you respond after making a mistake;
- you maintain your effort;
- you learn.

Reliability does not mean playing without mistakes.

It means your mistakes do not repeatedly come from lack of attention, preparation or understanding.

The actions that build that reliability change according to your role.

Some positions organise more.

Some create more space.

Some carry greater responsibility near the rim.

Some need to create threat without the ball.

All of them need to be reliable in the responsibilities that belong to them.

First become useful.

Then increase your impact.

---

## 4. DO NOT TRY TO LEARN EVERYTHING AT ONCE

One of the most common mistakes when you want to improve quickly is training too many different things.

Three-pointers one day.

Post moves the next.

Then ball handling.

Then a step-back.

Then a fadeaway.

Then some move you saw on social media.

The result can be a beautiful collection of mediocre skills.

Your development needs order.

### LEVEL 1 · BE USEFUL

Learn to fulfil your role without constantly needing the game to adapt to you.

Defend.

Move.

Occupy space well.

Communicate.

Run in transition.

Execute your off-ball responsibilities.

Do the simple actions that repeatedly appear in your position well.

### LEVEL 2 · CONTRIBUTE WITH CONTROL

Catch the ball in balance.

Protect it.

Read before acting.

Pass on time.

Finish simple advantages.

Shoot when appropriate.

Execute the actions that appear most often in your role with control.

### LEVEL 3 · CREATE OR EXTEND ADVANTAGES

Then add tools.

More reads.

More solutions.

More ability to punish different defensive responses.

More responsibility.

You do not need to reach the third level by abandoning the first two.

**First, control.**

**Then, effectiveness.**

**Finally, variety.**

---

## 5. KEEP YOUR BASKETBALL SIMPLE

A good action does not need to be complicated.

The goal is to make good decisions quickly.

Before you catch the ball, try to know:

**Where is the defense?**

**Where is the rim?**

**Where are your teammates?**

**What space is available?**

When you catch:

### If you have a clear advantage

Use it.

### If you can create a good advantage

Create it.

### If there is no advantage

Do not invent one.

Move the ball.

Move yourself.

Keep playing.

A possession does not need to become something extraordinary every time the ball reaches your hands.

**The difficulty of an action does not determine its quality.**

# CHAPTER 2 · BUILD YOUR GAME

## 6. LEARN TO PLAY WITH YOUR BODY

Having certain physical qualities and knowing how to use them to play are different things.

Height.

Strength.

Speed.

Wingspan.

Coordination.

Balance.

Explosiveness.

Laterality.

None of them automatically becomes an advantage.

You need to learn to use your body to:

- maintain balance;
- change pace;
- gain or protect space;
- absorb or avoid contact when appropriate;
- protect the ball;
- defend without unnecessary fouls;
- create good angles;
- control your movement.

You also need to understand your laterality.

If you mainly play with one hand, learn to recognise which angles and actions feel more natural and which ones you still need to develop.

Playing mainly with your left hand can create less familiar angles for some defenses.

That can be an advantage.

It should not become a dependency.

Likewise, defending someone who clearly prefers the left is not exactly the same as defending someone who prefers the right.

Watch their dominant hand, their driving angles and their actual tendencies.

Do not defend a label.

Whatever your preferred hand, developing the other one expands your solutions.

What it means to use your body well changes according to your position and the situations you face most often.

Your physical qualities are a tool.

**Learning how to use them is part of your technique.**

---

## 7. REBOUNDING IS A DECISION

A rebound does not begin when the ball touches the rim.

It begins when the shot goes up.

On defense, a basic sequence is:

**shot → opponent → position or contact → ball.**

Not:

> shot → watch ball → jump.

Finish your defensive responsibility first.

Then go after the ball.

Not every position has exactly the same rebounding responsibility.

Sometimes you need to box out your opponent.

Sometimes you need to attack the ball.

Sometimes you need to help finish the possession.

Sometimes you need to prepare to start or run in transition.

What you should never do is turn rebounding into a completely improvised reaction.

The opponent's shot does not end the possession.

**The rebound can.**

---

## 8. RUN

Transition begins before both teams are organised.

When possession changes, react.

### WE HAVE REGAINED POSSESSION

Immediately fulfil your first offensive responsibility.

That may be running a lane.

Spacing the floor.

Coming to the ball.

Running to the rim.

Providing an outlet.

Creating depth.

It depends on your role and the situation.

### WE HAVE LOST POSSESSION

React back.

Do not complain.

Do not dwell on the missed shot.

Do not look at the referee while the opponent runs.

Defend.

The previous play no longer exists.

Running well is not only about running fast.

It is about **getting to the right place early**.

---

## 9. UNDERSTAND SCREENS

A good screen can create a basket without appearing in any statistic.

But screens are not only the responsibility of the player setting them.

A screen is a coordinated action.

To take part in it well, you need to understand:

- the angle;
- the timing;
- the space;
- the speed;
- the position of the defense;
- what advantage the action is trying to create;
- what happens next.

### USE THE SCREEN TIGHTLY

If you are the player benefiting from a screen, do not leave a lane between your path and the screener.

Go **shoulder to shoulder**.

The more space you leave, the easier it is for your defender to slip through that gap and erase the advantage.

Closing that space is the responsibility of the player using the screen.

Do not expect the screener to move to close it for you. At the moment of contact, the screener must maintain a legal position. If they move to intercept or correct the defender's path, they can turn a good screen into an offensive foul.

This matters on both on-ball and off-ball screens.

**The screener creates the obstacle. The player using the screen must take advantage of it.**

Depending on your role, you will need to learn how to set screens, use them, reject them, change angles, roll, pop, re-screen or read the defensive reaction.

Do not execute the action as if it ends at the screen.

The goal is to create or extend an advantage.

After the first action:

**keep playing.**

---

## 10. TRAIN THE BORING FUNDAMENTALS

There are spectacular drills and important drills.

They are not always the same thing.

You do not need to discover a new drill every week.

You need to execute the movements you actually use better.

Fundamentals include:

- footwork;
- stops and pivots;
- catching;
- passing;
- ball handling;
- finishing;
- dominant and non-dominant hand;
- shooting;
- balance;
- coordination;
- defensive movement.

The importance of each one changes according to your role and stage of development.

You do not need to train all of them with the same priority.

You need to know which ones currently support your game.

Repetition without attention only accumulates repetitions.

Deliberate repetition has a different structure:

**do → observe → correct → repeat.**

During a game there is no time to rebuild every movement technically.

Good automatic habits free up more attention to read the game.

# CHAPTER 3 · LEARN EVERY DAY

## 11. LEARN BASKETBALL OFF THE COURT

Your game does not grow only through physical training.

You also need to learn how to **watch basketball**.

When you watch a game, do not always follow the ball.

Watch players who perform roles similar to yours.

Ask yourself:

- Where do they position themselves?
- When do they move?
- What do they look at before receiving?
- What do they do after passing?
- How do they create space?
- How do they react when the defense changes?
- What do they do in transition?
- How do they contribute when they do not have the ball?
- How do they help finish a defensive possession?
- What decisions do they repeat?

Do not copy only the moves.

Try to understand **why they appear**.

When you review video of your own games, do not try to fix twenty things at once.

Look for only:

### One thing you did well.

### One decision you could have made better.

### One thing you will work on next.

Learning requires focus.

---

## 12. KEEP A BASKETBALL NOTEBOOK

It can be a notebook, a note on your phone or your journal in Hoopjot.

After training or competing, you can think about five questions:

**1. What did we work on?**

**2. What do I understand better today than before?**

**3. What mistake did I repeat?**

**4. What was I corrected on?**

**5. What do I want to do better next time?**

You do not need to answer all five every time.

You do not need to write a report.

A rating and a short note can be enough if they capture something worth remembering.

Over time you will have something much more useful than a vague impression:

**a real record of your development.**

---

## 13. WHEN YOU ARE CORRECTED

Do not immediately explain why the mistake happened.

Listen.

Watch.

Ask if you do not understand.

Try to correct it on the next action.

Your response can be as simple as:

**“Got it.”**

Then do it.

The coaching staff watches something more important than whether you make mistakes:

**they watch whether you learn from them.**

A correction used well follows three steps:

**understand → try → adjust.**

---

## 14. AFTER A MISTAKE: NEXT PLAY

You will turn the ball over.

You will miss shots.

You will make bad decisions.

You will be late to some actions.

You will commit fouls.

There will be bad days.

That is basketball.

Your response should be immediate:

**NEXT PLAY.**

Do not turn one mistake into two more.

Do not lose the next possession complaining about the previous one.

Do not switch off.

Do not try to compensate for one bad action with an even harder one.

The ability to mentally return to the present is a competitive skill.

It can be trained too.

---

## 15. PRACTICE STARTS BEFORE PRACTICE

Arrive with time.

Prepare your equipment.

Do the appropriate pre-session routines.

Get mentally into the session.

If you have specific work prescribed by professionals in medicine, physiotherapy or strength and conditioning, integrate it according to their instructions.

Do not arrive at the last minute trying to get organised while the first explanation begins.

Your goal is simple:

**when practice starts, you are already in practice.**

---

# CHAPTER 4 · SUSTAIN THE PROCESS

## 16. SLEEP IS TRAINING

Training creates fatigue.

Recovery allows you to adapt.

That is why sleep is not time stolen from training.

It is part of it.

Try to keep reasonably regular schedules and protect your sleep especially when any of these increase:

- training load;
- travel;
- games;
- exams;
- work;
- other major demands.

Avoid making your phone the last thing you attend to every night.

If you experience excessive sleepiness for several days, persistent sleep problems or fatigue that does not go away, communicate it and seek appropriate guidance.

---

## 17. EAT TO TRAIN

Your body needs energy to:

- train;
- recover;
- compete;
- repair tissue;
- maintain muscle mass;
- study or work;
- maintain its normal functions.

Do not turn food into a fight against your body.

Do not pursue a specific weight on your own as a condition for performance.

A diet that looks healthy can still be insufficient for the load you are carrying.

Take care of basic habits:

- eat regularly;
- have enough energy available to train;
- include appropriate sources of carbohydrates and protein;
- eat fruit and vegetables;
- stay hydrated;
- arrive at sessions with energy;
- recover after exertion.

When you need individual recommendations about nutrition, body composition or performance, work with qualified professionals.

---

## 18. TAKE CARE OF YOUR BODY WHEN EVERYTHING IS GOING WELL

It is easy to pay attention to your body when something hurts.

The difficult part is maintaining good habits when everything seems to be working.

Strength.

Mobility.

Control.

Recovery.

Physical preparation.

Preventive habits.

They should not appear only when there is a problem.

If qualified professionals have prescribed specific work for you, integrate it according to their instructions.

Do not increase, remove or modify a rehabilitation or return-to-sport process on your own.

Prevention does not need to wait until something goes wrong.

**It is part of being available.**

---

## 19. DO NOT ADD TRAINING OUT OF GUILT

There will be days when you think:

> “I am not improving fast enough.”

And an apparently simple solution will appear:

do more.

More shots.

More running.

More gym work.

More drills.

More sessions.

Do not confuse **doing more** with **improving more**.

Before adding work, ask yourself:

- What exactly do I want to improve?
- Does this work address that objective?
- Am I recovered enough to do it?
- Is it part of the plan?
- Will it interfere with the next practice?
- Do I need more volume or do I need to execute better?

Extra work should have a purpose.

It should not be punishment for missing.

---

## 20. YOUR BODY IS NOT YOUR ENEMY

After an injury, a recovery process, a long period without competing or a bad physical experience, some movements can create doubt.

A jump.

Contact.

A fall.

A change of direction.

That does not mean you need to prove there is no fear.

If you are in a return-to-sport process, rebuild movements and load according to the criteria set by the professionals who know your case.

Physical confidence is also part of the return.

And the psychological side of that process matters.

If persistent fear, insecurity or lack of confidence appears, communicate it and seek appropriate professional support.

If you are not dealing with an injury or recovery process, do not turn this point into a problem that does not exist.

The idea is simpler:

**listen to your body without treating it as an enemy or using it as a constant test of toughness.**

---

# CHAPTER 5 · BE PART OF A TEAM

## 21. MAKE IT EASY TO PLAY WITH YOU

Your team should be able to rely on certain things.

If someone needs defensive help, you will try to get there.

If there is a better advantage, you will move the ball.

If a drive needs space, you will not close it off for your own convenience.

If your responsibility is to screen, cut, space the floor, organise, box out or create a passing lane, do it even if the action does not end with you.

If someone makes a mistake, do not spend the next possession blaming them for it.

If someone falls to the floor, help them up.

Basketball contains a lot of work that does not appear in the box score.

Your position will change some of that work.

The principle does not change:

**make playing with you help the team play better.**

---

## 22. TALK

Communication prevents mistakes before they happen.

Talk on defense.

In transition.

On screens.

On help.

On switches.

On cuts.

In special situations.

You do not need to be captain to communicate.

Not every position sees the same things or needs to communicate exactly the same information either.

Learn what information you can provide from your place on the court.

Talking well does not mean talking all the time.

It means providing information that is:

**useful, clear and on time.**

Five players sharing information react better than five players trying to guess.

---

## 23. KNOW THE SYSTEMS PERFECTLY

Do not let an avoidable tactical gap keep you off the court.

Learn:

- the plays;
- the positions;
- the variations;
- the defensive rules;
- the coverages;
- special situations;
- inbound plays;
- transition;
- the team's terminology.

If you do not understand something, ask.

If there is written material, study it.

If there is video, review it.

You can miss a shot while executing an action correctly.

It is much harder to justify being in the wrong place because you did not know what was supposed to happen.

Technique allows you to execute.

Tactical knowledge helps you know **what needs to be executed now**.

---

## 24. WHAT HAPPENS OFF THE COURT COUNTS TOO

Practices and games coexist with the rest of your life.

Studies.

Work.

Family.

Travel.

Rest.

Appointments.

Personal commitments.

Do not wait until everything becomes an emergency.

Use a calendar.

At the start of the week, identify what will genuinely shape your days.

When a particularly demanding week is coming, get ahead on what you can.

When there is a problem, communicate it early.

Planning does not make your obligations disappear.

It prevents all of them from arriving at the same time.

# CHAPTER 6 · GROW WITH PERSPECTIVE

## 25. YOUR WEEKLY SYSTEM

From time to time, you need to step back from today's practice and look at the whole process.

You can do a simple review.

If it helps, rate each area from 1 to 5:

| Area | Score |
| --- | --- |
| Sleep | /5 |
| Nutrition | /5 |
| Recovery | /5 |
| General physical condition | /5 |
| Concentration | /5 |
| Intensity | /5 |
| Responsibilities of your role | /5 |
| Fundamentals | /5 |
| Tactical knowledge | /5 |
| Communication and team | /5 |
| Off-court responsibilities | /5 |
| Mood | /5 |

Then answer only three questions:

### What am I doing well?

### What needs to improve?

### What will my priority be?

Only one priority.

Not six.

This review is a tool for perspective.

**It is not an administrative obligation.**

It does not need to become a weekly form, a streak, a progress score or another task you need to maintain to please an app.

Its value is in helping you think.

---

## 26. YOUR DEVELOPMENT PLAN

Your game should grow in stages.

Not because everyone develops at the same speed, but because trying to develop everything at once usually creates very little real mastery.

### PHASE 1 · RELIABILITY

Objective:

**Your coaching staff trusts putting you on the court.**

First, you need to fulfil the essential responsibilities of your role with enough consistency.

Know the systems.

Defend.

Take care of the ball.

Occupy space correctly.

Fulfil your off-ball responsibilities.

Make simple decisions.

### PHASE 2 · IMPACT

Objective:

**The team gets more value from having you on the court.**

Reliability is no longer only the absence of mistakes.

You begin to create, extend or preserve advantages more consistently.

You read better.

You execute earlier.

You help more.

You punish defensive mistakes better.

### PHASE 3 · EXPAND YOUR GAME

Objective:

**Add new solutions without losing the reliability you have built.**

More tools.

More reads.

More versatility.

More responsibility.

Do not move on simply because a new skill is more entertaining.

Move on when enough of the previous phase has started to become stable.

---

## 27. THE STATS THAT DO NOT SHOW UP

Do not measure your game only by points.

Points are visible.

That does not make them a complete measure of the game.

Some actions change possessions without clearly appearing in a traditional statistic.

A good screen.

A box out that allows a teammate to get the rebound.

A timely help rotation.

An advantage created with a pass.

Good spacing.

Communication that prevents a basket.

A run that forces the defense to react.

After playing, you can ask yourself:

- Did I make good decisions?
- Did I fulfil my defensive responsibilities?
- Did I help create or preserve advantages?
- Did I communicate?
- Did I execute my off-ball actions well?
- Did I repeat avoidable mistakes?
- Did some of my actions help the team play better?

The specific answers will depend on your role.

The principle is the same.

You can play well while scoring little.

You can play badly while scoring a lot.

**Learn the difference.**

---

## 28. WHEN YOU DO NOT PLAY

There will be games when you play little.

Maybe you will not play at all.

That does not turn the rest of the game into dead time.

Watch.

Encourage your teammates.

Identify what is working.

Listen to what the coaching staff is asking for.

Watch how the opponent is defending.

Stay ready.

When your minutes come, do not try to recover in thirty seconds everything you did not play.

Execute the first simple action that belongs to you correctly.

Then the next one.

Impatience is a very effective way to waste limited minutes.

---

## 29. DO NOT COMPARE YOURSELF WITH OTHERS EVERY DAY

There will always be someone:

- with more skill;
- with more experience;
- faster;
- stronger;
- with a better shot;
- with more minutes;
- with different opportunities.

You need to watch other people to learn.

You do not need to use every comparison as a judgement on your future.

You cannot compress someone else's years of experience into a few weeks.

The most useful comparison needs time.

Look back a few months.

Do you understand more?

Do you decide earlier?

Do you defend better?

Is your footwork better?

Do you read advantages better?

Do you repeat fewer mistakes?

Have you added solutions?

Are you more reliable?

That is progress.

---

## 30. YOUR BIGGEST ADVANTAGE

Your biggest advantage does not have to be a specific physical quality.

It can be something else:

**your ability to improve.**

If you still have a lot of basketball to learn, every practice contains something you can add.

Listen better.

Observe better.

Practice with intention.

Correct.

Try again.

Turn frequent mistakes into occasional mistakes.

Turn conscious actions into automatic habits.

Turn automatic habits into tools that remain available when the game speeds up.

You do not need to constantly prove everything you can already do.

Work to discover how far your game can grow.

When everything above feels too long, remember this:

### 2. Arrive fully prepared.

### 3. Listen to your coaching staff.

### 4. Learn the systems.

### 5. Defend.

### 6. Box out.

### 7. Run the floor.

### 8. Make the simple things easy.

### 9. After a mistake: next play.

### 10. Make your team better.

### 11. Improve something every week.

### 12. Be consistent when nobody is watching.

# ONE LAST IDEA

Making a team is not the objective.

Being on a roster is not either.

Not even playing in a game.

The real opportunity is having months of practices, coaching staff, teammates, gym work, competition and hundreds of situations in which you can become a better player.

You do not need to know today what your ceiling will be.

You need to use the next opportunity to improve.
